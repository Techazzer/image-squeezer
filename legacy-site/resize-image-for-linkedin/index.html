LinkedIn image size guide 2025

LinkedIn accepts images up to 8MB for profile photos, but the platform re-compresses everything to its own standard. Uploading a well-compressed photo (400–600KB) actually produces a sharper result than uploading a raw 8MB file, because LinkedIn's own compression is more aggressive when given a large input file.

How to prepare your LinkedIn profile photo (4 steps)

  1. Use a recent, professional photo: good lighting, plain background (white or light grey), you facing the camera. No group photos, no filters. Your face should fill 60–70% of the frame.

  2. If your photo is already square and well-cropped, upload it above. Target is pre-set to 500KB — a good size that LinkedIn handles well. Click Compress.

  3. Review the preview. The photo should look sharp and professional at small sizes. Click Download.

  4. On LinkedIn: go to your profile → click your photo → Add photo → upload → use LinkedIn's crop tool to centre your face, then save.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does LinkedIn accept PNG for profile photos?

Yes — LinkedIn accepts both JPEG and PNG. For photos (human faces), JPEG produces better compression at equivalent quality. PNG is better for logos with transparency. For a profile photo, JPEG at 400–600KB is ideal.

My photo is 8MB from my DSLR — do I need to resize it?

You can upload it directly (LinkedIn accepts up to 8MB), but we recommend compressing first. A raw DSLR file is often 6000×4000px — LinkedIn crops it to a square and re-compresses aggressively, which can cause quality loss. Giving LinkedIn a clean 400×400px, 500KB JPEG produces a sharper result.

Why does my LinkedIn profile photo look blurry after upload?

Two common causes: (1) you uploaded a photo smaller than 400×400px and it was upscaled, or (2) you uploaded a very large file that LinkedIn over-compressed. Use a clean 400–800px wide JPEG at 400–600KB for best results.

Can I use this tool to compress my LinkedIn banner image too?

Yes. Upload your banner (1584×396px recommended), set a custom target if needed, and compress. A 1584×396px JPEG at around 300–500KB looks sharp on all devices and loads fast.
